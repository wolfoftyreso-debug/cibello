Best before and use by: what the dates on food really mean

Two short phrases on a packet cause a surprising amount of food to be thrown away. Best before is a quality mark; use by is a safety limit. Knowing the difference, and having a way to remember what is due when, saves food and money without taking any risk. Here is what the dates mean and how to keep track of them.

Best before: a quality date

Best before states how long the producer guarantees full quality: taste, texture and nutritional value. After the date, the food is often still perfectly good to eat, provided it has been stored properly and the packaging is unopened. That applies to milk, yoghurt, eggs, hard cheese, tinned goods, dry staples and frozen food. Look, smell and taste: national food safety authorities across Europe describe that as a sensible way to judge food past its best before date. Across the EU, and in the UK, the two phrases are set by food information law, so the wording means the same thing wherever you shop.

Use by: a safety date

Use by appears on perishable foods where bacteria can multiply without the food smelling or looking wrong, for instance fresh chicken, mince, fresh fish and some ready meals. After that date the food should not be eaten, even if it seems fine. The safe option is to cook or freeze it on or before the date. Freezing stops the clock, and a frozen item can be cooked after defrosting. Once a pack is opened, follow the storage instructions on it, which usually shorten the time you have regardless of the printed date.

Storing food so the dates hold

Dates assume correct storage. A fridge at 5 degrees or below, milk and meat on the lower shelves rather than in the door, and opened jars covered and chilled all extend the useful life of what you buy. Bread, onions and potatoes prefer a cool cupboard to the fridge. Eggs keep longer in the fridge than at room temperature. None of this is complicated; the hard part is remembering what is in there and when it went in.

Why the confusion costs so much

A large share of the food thrown away at home was still fine to eat: yoghurt binned on the best before date, eggs discarded a week after it, bread that could have been frozen. Treating best before as a hard stop is an easy habit to fall into when you are tired and the date is printed in bold. Knowing that it is a quality date, and checking the food rather than the number, is a change that saves a great deal of food on its own. The second change is having a system that tells you what to use first, so the check happens before the food turns rather than after.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between best before and use by?

Best before is a quality date: the food is often fine to eat afterwards if it has been stored properly. Use by is a safety date for perishable foods such as chicken and mince, and should not be passed.

Can you eat food after the best before date?

Often, yes. Look, smell and taste. This does not apply to food marked with a use by date.

How long does milk keep after the best before date?

Often a few days up to a week if it has stayed unopened and cold. Smell and taste decide.

Can an app remind me before food goes off?

Yes. Cibello keeps dates in your food inventory, reminds you when something should be used and suggests recipes that use it. Always check the dates yourself.
